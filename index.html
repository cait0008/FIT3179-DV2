Digital Inclusion in Australia

9 October 2026

Section 1

Introduction to the digital divide

Banking, healthcare, school and government services have all moved online, so being unable to get online now means being shut out of everyday life. The Australian Digital Inclusion Index (ADII) measures how well Australians can take part online across three dimensions: Access (having a good connection and device), Affordability (being able to pay for it) and Digital Ability (having the skills to use it).

This page uses the 2025 ADII to ask four questions: who is left behind, where they live, why, and whether the gap is closing.

Section 2

How big is the digital divide?

Australia scores 73.6 out of 100 on the ADII, which sounds fine until you look at who sits underneath that number. About one in five Australians is digitally excluded, and for First Nations Australians it is closer to two in five. Before looking at who those people are, it helps to know what is being measured.

The ADII Score

Each bar is a national score out of 100, and each colour stays with its dimension for the rest of the page. The overall ADII is simply the average of the three. Access is the strongest part (76.8), while Affordability is the weakest (70.3), so before looking at any group it is worth knowing that cost is already the soft spot for the country as a whole.

The digital divide between all Australians and First Nations Australians

Unlike the score charts, this counts people: each grid is 100 Australians, coloured from highly excluded (dark pink) to highly included (dark grey). About 21 in 100 Australians are excluded, compared with about 41 in 100 First Nations Australians. The gap is sharpest at the very bottom, where 23 in 100 First Nations people are highly excluded, about 2.5 times the national rate.

Section 3

Who faces this divide?

Digital exclusion is not spread evenly. It piles up in particular groups: older Australians, people who left school early, people without work, people with disability and First Nations people. These charts start with how many people in each group are excluded, then look at how far each group falls behind on access to the internet.

How many people in each group are digitally excluded

This extends the waffle to every group. Bars to the left of zero are the share of the group who are excluded, bars to the right are included, and the dashed line marks the national 21%. Anything reaching past that line is worse off than average. Two in three people aged 75+ (67%) and over half of people who did not finish school (55%) are excluded, compared with only 6% of 18 to 34 year olds and of people with a degree.

Access to the internet: each group vs the national average

Where the bar chart counts people, this shows scores, and only for Access. Each dot is a group's score and the line runs to the national average, so a longer line means a bigger gap. About half the groups sit within 5 points of average, which makes the outliers stand out: First Nations people in very remote areas score just 37.3, about half the national score, followed by First Nations people in remote areas (55.9) and very remote Australians generally (57.4).

Section 4

Does where you live correlate with digital exclusion?

Where you live matters too. Remote and very remote communities score lowest, but most local council areas also sit below the national average, because a handful of big city councils pull it up. The first two maps show how far behind each area is. The cartogram then resizes every area by population, so you can see where the affected people actually live rather than how much land they cover.

How far behind the average is each local area?

This moves from groups to places. Each spike is a council that scores below the national ADII, and its height shows how far below, so you can compare size of gap, not just whether a place is behind. Use the buttons to zoom into a state. The tallest spikes are not spread evenly: they cluster in the Top End and Arnhem Land, Cape York and the Torres Strait, and the Kimberley, which are remote areas with large First Nations populations.

Which local areas score above or below average?

The same councils, simplified to above (green) or below (purple) the national average. The map looks overwhelmingly purple: 423 of 545 councils fall below average, and the green is mostly squeezed into small patches around the capital cities. But each shape shows land, not people, so a huge outback council with a few thousand residents takes up far more space than a city council with hundreds of thousands. The next chart corrects for that.

Comparing these ADII scores through population

Here every council is a circle sized by its population, so empty land shrinks and cities grow. Use the buttons to zoom into a state. The picture changes completely from the map above: only 122 councils score at or above average, but they are home to 56% of Australians, because big city councils like Brisbane, Blacktown and the ACT are mostly green. At the other end, the 80 councils more than 10 points below average hold under 1% of the population. The worst-off places are real, but they are small and remote, which makes them easy to overlook.

Section 5

Why are certain groups excluded?

Knowing who and where is only half the story. To help, you need to know what is actually holding people back: a poor connection, the cost, or not having the skills. The answer changes from group to group. Remote areas mostly struggle with connections, while for many groups the bigger gap is skills rather than money. The last chart asks people directly what stops them from going online more.

Causes of the digital divide

The dumbbell only showed Access. This puts all three dimensions side by side for every group, with purple for below average, green for above, and the score written in each cell. Reading across a row shows whether a group is behind everywhere or on just one thing. People aged 75+ are behind on all three, but by far the most on Digital Ability (32 points below). Remote First Nations groups are furthest behind on Access instead.

The impact of remoteness on Access, affordability and ability

The heatmap showed remoteness as rows. Here it is mapped, with one panel per dimension on the same colour scale, so the panels can be compared directly. Access falls away sharply with distance (very remote areas are 19 points below). Affordability barely changes (under 4 points), and remote areas actually score better than regional ones on both Affordability and Digital Ability, so distance mainly hurts connections, not cost or skills.

The impact of money and skills on digitally excluded groups

This boils each group down to one number: its skills gap minus its money gap. Bars to the left (indigo) mean skills are the bigger problem, and bars to the right (terracotta) mean cost is. The groups split almost evenly (16 lean towards skills, 13 towards money), which shows there is no single fix. Older Australians lean strongly towards skills, while unemployed people and urban First Nations people lean towards cost. Even young people aged 18 to 34, who are above average on both, have more room to improve on cost.

Closer analysis of the relationship between skills and money

A closer look at seven key groups from the chart above, showing the actual gaps rather than just the difference. Each line runs from the money gap (left) to the skills gap (right), so a line that drops means skills are the bigger barrier and a line that rises means cost is. Aged 75+ drops steeply, from 19 points below on money to 32 below on skills. Unemployed people rise, from 14 below on money to slightly above average on skills.

What stops people getting online?

The charts above are built from ADII scores. This one shows what people say themselves, as the share of each group who report each barrier (people could choose more than one). Nationally, concern about privacy and scams (15%) and slow connections (14%) top the list, and cost comes in at only 6%. The outlined cells match the patterns above: older Australians often say the internet is not a priority (29%), remote areas report slow connections (29%), and cost matters most to unemployed people (15%).

Section 6

Is the gap closing?

The good news is that scores have gone up in every survey since 2020. The harder question is whether the groups at the bottom are catching up, or just rising along with everyone else. Tracking each group over time, and its rank each year, gives a fairly clear answer.

Comparison of the digital divide over time

Each line tracks how far a group sits below the national average in each survey, so a line moving up towards zero means the gap is closing. Use the buttons to switch between Access and Digital Ability, and note there was no survey in 2023. The trend is mixed. People with disability have closed in on both measures, and the skills gap for people aged 75+ narrowed from 39 to 32 points. But their Access gap has not moved (about 16 points), and the First Nations Access gap widened from 8 to 12.5 points.

What goups remain excluded over time?

The chart above shows the size of each gap. This one shows rank, from 1 (highest score) down, so it answers a simpler question: has anyone changed places? Groups in the middle shuffle a little each year, and the dashed national average sits mid-table. At the bottom, nothing changes: people aged 75+ and people who did not finish secondary school are last and second-last in every survey since 2020, on both Access and Digital Ability.

Section 7

Conclusion

Digital exclusion in Australia is not random. It is concentrated in older Australians, people who left school early, people with disability and First Nations people, and it is deepest in remote communities. The causes are different for each of them. Remote areas mostly lack good connections, older Australians mostly lack the skills and see less reason to go online, and for people without work the main problem is cost.

Scores are improving across the country, but the same groups are still at the bottom every year. Rising averages alone will not close the gap. That will take help aimed at the right people, in the right places, for the right reason: better connections in remote areas, skills support for older Australians, and affordable plans for people on low incomes.

How the data was prepared

The 41 ADII 2025 dashboard downloads (one per theme and population split) were combined into five workbooks, one per theme, with every group stacked in a single table. Duplicates were removed and tables reshaped, but no values were changed. Python scripts then made a small CSV for each chart, joined LGA scores to the ABS boundaries and 2025 populations by LGA code, and worked out the cartogram layout in advance. Boundaries were converted to TopoJSON. Claude (Anthropic, 2026) helped write these scripts; outputs were checked against the source data. Charts are adapted from Vega-Lite gallery examples (Satyanarayan et al., 2017).
